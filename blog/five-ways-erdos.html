Five Ways to Measure Closeness to a Hungarian Mathematician

8 October 2026

I'm talking about Erdős. I'm a mathematician, who else? If you just want to skip to the data, it's here. I first learned about Erdős in the book The Man Who Loved Only Numbers by Paul Hoffman. It was the first popular math book I read (I think), but it clicked with me in a way that few books do. Now that I'm studying combinatorics, my obsession (hyperfixation, whatever) is renewed.

I created this ranking to try to measure how Erdős-coded some conferences I went to are. After attending Peter Winkler's birthday conference and hearing about other conferences, I wanted a way to quantify how close they were to Erdős's interests, and by proxy mine.

I have since used it to look at what schools have Hungarian-style combinatorics and the like, but mostly I like the data for the data's sake. For various reasons coding is hard on my headaches, so all of the coding and web scraping was done by Claude.

1) The Erdős number (the obvious one)

The Erdős number is a metric that represents collaboration distance among mathematicians. You have an Erdős number of 1 if you collaborated with Erdős, 2 if you collaborated with someone who collaborated with Erdős, etc. Erdős is one of, if not the, most prolific mathematicians of the 20th century, with 515 coauthors and about 1500 papers (and counting, though he is dead!). Anyway, this has been deeply studied and cataloged.

1b) Fractional Erdős numbers

Complete lists are only available for ppl with numbers 1 and 2. My only additional thought here is that it's not continuous enough. Ron Graham and someone who only wrote one paper with Erdős shouldn't get the same Erdős number. So I think your Erdős number should be e − 1 + 1/n, where e is the usual Erdős number and n is the number of papers you wrote with ppl whose Erdős number is e − 1. If my Erdős number is 3 due to one paper, this recovers 3 = 3 − 1 + 1/1. The idea is that the ppl who understand Erdős most wrote the most papers with him. The people who understand what it is like to collaborate with Erdős have the most Erdős collaborators and so forth.

I came up with this idea independently, but there's a very similar treatment by Michael Barr, Rational Erdős numbers. I think, however, this is the least interesting idea; we are just getting started.

2) The second least interesting idea

After the obvious one, my idea for measuring closeness to Erdős was number of papers. However, I quickly realized that this is a poor metric. It mostly rewards having been in the room: people who were around in some golden age, like the 70s, when Erdős was at the top of his game. That of course produces an interesting list of mathematicians, but it doesn't say much about who's working like Erdős now. Anyway, people have already considered this.

4) Problems!

Problems! That's the other thing we are missing. Erdős cared deeply about posing and stating problems.

Erdős had a way of finding problems that define fields, delineate the difference between hard and impossible, and give mathematicians something to strive for. So using Thomas Bloom's Erdős problems database we can measure how many problems someone has solved or made progress on (half credit) and rank people that way. If you like, you can look at fractional credit — divide solves by authors and see who solves the most alone or together.

5) The full ranking

So we now have two rankings, one that measures collaboration and one that measures numbers of problems solved. Now of course we want to combine the two and have some overall ranking. So my idea is to normalize by the best human on each list, then multiply by 50, to get a score out of 100. This sets our balance parameter α to 0.5: 50% collab, 50% problems. If you think this overweights collaboration, you can mess around with α in the sliders at the bottom and look at the distribution.

Now we have made a way to measure closeness to Erdős. To be clear, this isn't a metric of who's the best mathematician. Szemerédi and Alon are both incredibly talented, but they rank high because they work on Erdős-like math. My advisor Miklós Bóna is very talented but by and large does enumerative combo, which Erdős didn't do much of. So my advisor gets a zero on the problem criterion.

4b) Not all problems are equal

My next thought was not all Erdős problems are created equal. Some, like the bounds on diagonal Ramsey numbers, were open for many years before people made progress, and lots of people thought about them; others languished, forgotten in papers somewhere. So we want a multiplier for how important / hard / cared-about a given problem is.

So we have a scale from 1 to 5. Maybe the scale should be exponential, but this is simpler.

  • 5 — Extreme importance, long open, big money, people have been working on it
  • 3 — Some people have worked on it, some money
  • 1 — people haven't been working on it, no money

I started with this 1–5 system as a guideline to rank problems, but the final scores are decimals with 2 digits, not integers.

Some examples of the scale
ScoreProblemPrize
5.00#165: an asymptotic formula for R(3,k). Open.$250
4.06#139: sets with no k-term arithmetic progression have density zero. Szemerédi's theorem.$1000
3.39#634: for which n can some triangle be cut into n congruent triangles? Open.$25
1.39#725: an asymptotic formula for the number of k × n Latin rectangles. Open.—
1.00#405: for an odd prime p, does (p−1)! + ap−1 = pk have only finitely many solutions? Proved.—

It's worth noting we used time as a tie-breaker / multiplier. If nobody cared about a problem 80 years ago, it's still probably a 1, but if people have cared about it for 80 years and it's still unsolved, like the R(k,k) lower bound, then it's probably important. Time is an important proxy for attention and hardness, but only if people are looking.

Anyway, the 3 things that go into the ranking are prize, attention and time. The caveat is that attention is based on comments on blogs, likes, the Erdős problems website, papers, etc.

Ok, so you probably look at Latin rectangles (#725 above: count the k × n Latin rectangles) and think it's likely not a 1.39, but it has little attention on the Erdős problems website. As the people at the design theory summer school EXCILL V would probably tell you, this is a somewhat important problem: it counts a basic object in their field. So my guess is it's likely a 3.

I think this system will usually underestimate important problems, with a very few overestimated due to recency bias and recent solves. If you are interested in seeing how various AI solves rank on this scale, email me. I think this could be a post in its own right so I'm not going to talk about it here.

Now you've probably looked at these lists and seen the names you expect — Alon, Graham, Szemerédi, Tao. And you think hmm, someone I would expect, cough cough Lovász, is conspicuously missing. That is in fact not a mistake. He is 29th on the combined ranking, mostly cause his skill is coming up with new tools. He only has 4.76 Erdős problem points. But he makes tools: he used topological tools to prove the Kneser conjecture and founded the field of topological combinatorics, and his Local Lemma has surely solved or made progress on a number of problems. Regardless, there are many ways to be good at math and honor Erdős, and finding new tools is a great one, but this metric doesn't measure that.

Notes

The original title was "5 ways to measure closeness to a dead Hungarian," but I decided to change it.

We don't count Erdős problems solved by AI, because we don't know how to score them.

The Erdős problem numbers are soft, since it is sometimes hard to distinguish the papers that pose a problem from the ones that solve it.

All of the problem data comes from Thomas Bloom's erdosproblems.com, which this post could not exist without. The site is changing format, so this ranking won't be possible to recompute for much longer.
